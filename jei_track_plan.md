# Planning toward the Journal of Emerging Investigators (JEI)

Source: a subagent read nine pages of emerginginvestigators.org and reported back. I have not read the pages myself, so **confirm anything that matters on the official site**. Pages it could not read are listed at the end.

## 1. Two things you need to know before anything else

### A. JEI bans AI help with most of the work, including parts I have already done
Per the JEI page on academic honesty and AI (as reported to me), JEI bans AI for: reading, summarizing or analyzing published studies; writing text or revisions; making images; statistical analysis, modeling or calculations; data mining; and building reference lists. AI proofreading for spelling and grammar after the text is written is the allowed use. Violations can get a manuscript rejected or withdrawn publicly, and serious ones can lead to bans.

What this means for this project:
- **The prior-art summaries and plan I wrote are planning aids, not material for your paper.** Do not copy my summaries into your introduction. Read the cited papers yourself and write your own account. My citation lists are only a reading list, and I read search summaries, not full papers, so some details may be wrong.
- **Do the statistics and calculations yourself** (spreadsheet or a stats tool you run and understand). I should not run your analysis if this goes to JEI.
- **Write the manuscript yourself.** I can explain what a section is for, but I should not draft or revise your text.
- **The hypothesis must be yours.** JEI says the student must devise the hypothesis and experiments. The research questions in my plan were my suggestions. Treat them as options to think about, then write your own in your own words.
- Whether AI use must be disclosed is not stated on the pages I was given. Check.

### B. The project as I framed it may be out of scope
JEI accepts hypothesis-driven natural science (life sciences and experimental physical sciences). It does **not** accept, among other things: manuscripts that only introduce, optimize or compare an invention, model, method or algorithm; descriptive or data-mining work; computer science; or work with no clear hypothesis. Tools such as new methods may be used to test a hypothesis but cannot be the subject of it.

My plan is a benchmark of fast self-discharge tests. Written as "which screen is best", it sits in the excluded category. The open-hardware and dataset parts also do not count as the science.

**Direction that may fit:** make the subject a physical question about the cells, with the rig and the fast tests as your measuring tools. Examples to think about (you choose and word your own):
- What determines how much charge a used cell leaks, and does a short measurement of that leakage track it over 28 days?
- Does leakage current rise in a predictable way when you add a known external leak path (a resistor) to a healthy cell?
- Do cells with different storage or usage history leak differently, and is that visible in a short test?

**Risk:** an editor may still judge it a method comparison. I cannot settle that from here. **Before you spend weeks of data collection, ask JEI** whether a hypothesis-driven study like this is in scope. I did not find a contact address, and the FAQ and "common mistakes" pages were not read, so start there.

## 3. Safety note on a parent as senior author
A parent can be the senior author if the work is done at home. That does not make home lithium-cell testing safe or approved. Keep the safety plan in section 8 of the research plan: fire-resistant container, no abuse tests, rejected cells to a recycler. If your parent is not comfortable supervising it, find another adult or use a school or lab setting.

## 4. Changes to the research plan if you go to JEI

1. **One primary hypothesis**, written by you before data collection, plus any secondary questions labelled as secondary. JEI wants a clear hypothesis, not four parallel questions.
2. **You collect the data.** A mentor can supervise and advise but should not run the experiments.
3. **Frozen before unblinding:** post your hypothesis, measurements and analysis plan publicly (for example OSF) before you look at the 28-day results.
4. **Controls and statistics** you can explain and run yourself. Include error bars on replicated data.
5. **Report the false-negative rate and any negative result.** JEI requires honest reporting even if the hypothesis is not supported.
6. **Open data and hardware** (Zenodo, GitHub for code) stay as an extra. They support reproducibility but are not the paper's science.
7. **Methods detailed enough for someone else to repeat.** This also helps the open-hardware goal.

## 5. Schedule toward JEI
JEI has no deadline I found. It handles manuscripts in the order received. Work backwards from your own readiness:

| Phase | Time | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| Decide scope: ask JEI, settle the hypothesis yourself | Weeks 0–2 | JEI's answer in hand and hypothesis frozen and posted |
| Mentor and safety approvals | Weeks 0–2 | Senior author agreed, safety plan signed off |
| Build and validate rig (gate G1) | Weeks 1–3 | Rig agrees with the borrowed instrument |
| Data collection (28-day reference plus screens) | Weeks 4–9 | All readings logged |
| Your analysis (done by you) | Weeks 9–10 | Figures with error bars |
| Draft in the JEI template (written by you) | Weeks 10–13 | Full draft, reference list from papers you read |
| Mentor review, fee or scholarship, submit | Weeks 13–14 | Adult submits |
| JEI review | Unknown | Timeline page not read |

## 6. Questions for you
1. Which grade are you in, and are you 13 or older?
2. Would a parent or another adult serve as senior author, and be comfortable supervising battery work?
3. Are you willing to write the hypothesis, analysis and manuscript yourself, given JEI's AI rules? If not, JEI is the wrong outlet and the science-fair or open-release routes fit better.
